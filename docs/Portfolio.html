Description

The auto_claim data set comes from the SOA’s Predictive Analytics Exam practice materials, it contains 10296 observations and 29 variables. This data was collected in order to promote predictive analytics education within an actuarial context. Each observation is an individual auto insurance policy. Some of the variables include demographic information of the policy holder such as age, gender, income, and education. Other variables include characteristics about the vehicle such as car type, blue book value, and car use. The response variable that is being investigated in this analysis will be the CLM_FLAG variable which indicates whether or not a policy has made a claim. About 27% of policies have a claim, while 73% do not. We will be exploring which policy holder and vehicle characteristics are associated with a higher likelihood of filing a claim.

Before splitting the data, missing values in INCOME, HOME_VAL, YOJ, and SAMEHOME were imputed using median imputation, preserving all 10296 observations. The data was then split in to a training set(80%, n = 8236) and a test set(20%, n = 2060). A stratified sample was used to ensure that the ratio of policies with claims vs policies with no claims were roughly equal between the two sets. The size of the each set is appropriate since there is ample data to train the models with while keeping enough data left over to test on.

Section 3: Unsupervised Learning

Principal Component Analysis is a technique that aims to reduce the dimensionality of a large set of correlated variables in to a smaller set of uncorrelated variables called principal components. Each component is a linear combination of the original variables constructed in such a way that captures as much variation in the data as possible. The first component explains the most variation, the second component explains the second most variance, and so on. In this analysis, PCA was performed on 9 numeric variables from the set: AGE, INCOME, HOME_VAL, BLUEBOOK, TRAVTIME, MVR_PTS, NPOLICY, HOMEKIDS, and KIDSDRIV. All variables were standardized to ensure they contribute equally to the analysis.

Logistic Regression

Logistic regression is an extension of linear regression used to predict binary outcomes. In this case, it is whether a claim was filed or not. Regular linear regression predicts a continuous number, but we need a probability between 0 and 1. Logistic regression solves this by taking the linear combination of predictors and passing it through a sigmoid function, which constricts any value into a range between 0 and 1. Rather than a binary output of 0 or 1, the model outputs how likely it is that a claim was filed. If that predicted probability exceeds 50%, the policyholder is classified as Yes, otherwise No.

The model works by finding the best set of coefficients, one for each predictor, that maximizes the likelihood of correctly classifying the observed outcomes in the training data. Each coefficient tells us the direction and strength of a predictor’s relationship with claim likelihood. For example, a positive coefficient on MVR points means that more points increases the predicted probability of a claim, while a negative coefficient on age means higher age decreases it. This makes logistic regression one of the most straightforward classification models available since it gives you a readable equation that explains exactly how each variable influences the prediction.

Lasso regularization was used to automatically penalize unimportant predictors in the model. This method simplifies the model while also preventing over fitting on the training data. The penalty in this case is referred to as ‘lambda’. The higher lambda is, the higher the penalization is on the predictor variables, which results in a simpler model that may miss more patterns(more bias). A lower lambda fits the model more closely to the training data, but could result in over fitting(more variance). Higher variance means the model is more sensitive to changes in the training data, which could lead to model making poor predictions when presented with new data.The optimal lambda selected was 0.00234, this is a relatively small value which indicates that most of the predictors were useful.

Random Forest

In a random forest model, many decision trees are trained on a slightly different random sample (with replacement) of the data, which is a technique called bootstrapping. This method ensures each tree learns from a slightly different version of the data, making the trees diverse and less likely to all make the same mistakes. At every split in a tree, the model only considers a random subset of predictors rather than all of them, which is the key difference between a random forest and simply building many decision trees. Without this restriction, most trees would tend to use the same strong predictors at every split, making them too similar to each other and reducing the benefit of combining them.

The number of predictors considered at each split is controlled by the hyperparameter mtry. Each tree then makes a prediction on a new data point by passing it through its series of yes/no questions, and the prediction that receives the most votes across all trees becomes the final prediction. This approach is powerful because even if individual trees make errors, those errors tend to cancel out when combined across hundreds of trees, resulting in a more stable and accurate model than any single tree could achieve on its own

Examining the confusion matrices reveals an important limitation of both models. The logistic regression correctly identified only 205 out of 548 actual claims in the test set, missing 343. Meaning the model failed to categorize 63% of the policies with claims as high risk. The random forest performed similarly, missing 344 out of 548 claims. Both models are conservative in predicting claims, likely because the training data has nearly three times as many non-claim policies as claim policies, so the models learn that predicting ‘No’ is usually correct.

From the perspective of an insurance company, this is a significant concern since each high risk observation that was classified as low risk represents a policyholder that was likely underpriced due to their underestimated risk exposure. While accuracy is roughly 79% between the two models, this is largely driven by correctly identifying the larger group of policyholders who have not made claims. A possible solution to this would be adjusting the classification threshold below 50%, resulting in more false positives in exchange for catching more real claims.

To address the high false negative rate, the classification threshold was lowered from 50% to 30%. This means any policy with a predicted claim probability above 30% is flagged as high risk. With this threshold, the logistic regression correctly identified 392 out of 548 actual claims compared to only 205 at the default threshold, nearly doubling the number of claims caught. The random forest showed similar improvement, catching 393 claims. However this comes at the cost of an increased amount of false positives from 87 to 373 for logistic regression, meaning more low risk policyholders are incorrectly flagged as high risk and potentially overcharged. The optimal threshold ultimately depends on how the insurer weighs the cost of missing a claim against the cost of overcharging a low risk customer.

Conclusion and Insights

This analysis sets out to identify which policyholder and vehicle characteristics are most associated with an increased likelihood of filing an auto insurance claim. The exploratory data analysis revealed driving the history variables, like license revocation status and vehicle record points, were the strongest predictors of claim likelihood. These predictors are followed by vehicle characteristics such as car type and car use. Demographic factors such as age and education levels also showed meaningful patterns, with younger drivers and those with less education filing claims at higher rates.

Both the logistic regression and random forest models achieved similar test set accuracies of approximately 79%, suggesting that the simpler logistic regression model was sufficient to capture the relationships in this dataset. However, accuracy alone is not the best measure of success in an insurance context. Correctly identifying high risk policyholders matters more than overall correctness, and both models struggled to catch the majority of actual claims at the default threshold. Therefore, lowering the classification threshold to 30%, reducing false negatives at the cost of increased false positives, is a logical step to take in this scenario.

This analysis has several notable limitations. First, both models predict a binary outcome: whether a claim was filed or not. This is a simplification of how risk is treated in an insurance setting. In reality, policyholders vary not just in whether they file a claim but in how frequently they file claims and how severe those claims are. A binary classification model treats a policyholder who files one minor claim the same as one who files multiple large claims, missing important distinctions the risk levels of different policyholders.

Several extensions of this analysis would be valuable in a real world actuarial context. Rather than predicting binary outcomes, models could predict claim frequency and claim severity seperately. This would give the insurer a better picture of the overall risk they are exposed to. Beyond predictions, models could be used to rank a policyholder’s risk from low to high. This would aid underwriters in pricing new applicant’s based on their risk profile. Such a system would allow insurers to more precisely match a policyholder’s premium to their risk level, thus reducing the cross-subsubsidiaztion of premiums among policy holders.

Overall, this analysis demonstrates that auto insurance claim likelihood can be predicted with decent accuracy using policyholder and vehicle characteristics alone. These findings serve as a strong foundation for building more sophisticated models that could price premiums accurately based on customer data.
